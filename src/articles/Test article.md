Ask a student in Durham why rents are high and you will get an answer about landlords. Ask an economist and you will get an answer about supply. Both are describing the same market, but only one of them explains why the problem repeats every single year.

The mechanics are unglamorous. The number of students grows; the number of beds does not grow at the same rate. Purpose-built accommodation takes years to plan and build, and the housing stock of a small historic city cannot expand quickly. When demand rises against constrained supply, prices rise. Landlord behaviour determines how that surplus is divided — not whether it exists.

This matters because it changes what a solution looks like.

> Campaigns about landlord conduct can win real improvements. What they cannot do is lower the market price, because the price is set by the shortage.

The uncomfortable implication is that effective interventions are slow and unpopular. Building more accommodation works, but takes years and is usually opposed by people already living nearby. Capping rents helps current tenants while reducing what is available to future ones. Expanding university-owned housing shifts who captures the surplus without adding much to the total.

None of this means nothing can be done. It means the honest conversation is about which trade-off to accept, and over what timescale — not about finding an intervention with no costs attached.

Students are unusually well placed to make that argument. They experience the shortage directly and leave before any long-term fix arrives. That combination produces clear sight of the problem, and very little incentive to pretend the easy answers work.
