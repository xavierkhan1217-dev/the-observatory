There are two accounts of what a degree does. On the human capital view, education makes people more productive: graduates earn more because they can do more. On the signalling view, education mostly reveals productivity that was already there: graduates earn more because completing a degree demonstrates traits employers value and cannot observe directly.

Both accounts predict the same correlation between education and earnings. That is what makes the disagreement so persistent.

## Why the distinction matters

If education is primarily productive, public subsidy is straightforward: it raises output and the returns are partly social. If it is primarily a signal, subsidy funds an expensive sorting mechanism, and expanding access may simply raise the level of qualification required to send the same signal — credential inflation, with real resources consumed to no aggregate gain.

> On the pure signalling account, everyone studying an extra year leaves everyone in exactly the same relative position, poorer by a year.

## The evidence is genuinely mixed

- **Sheepskin effects** — the earnings jump at completion rather than accumulating smoothly per year — are usually read as evidence for signalling, though they are also consistent with the final year mattering disproportionately.
- **Natural experiments** from changes in compulsory schooling tend to find real returns to additional years, supporting the human capital side.
- **Field-of-study variation** complicates both: returns differ enormously by subject, which pure signalling struggles to explain and pure human capital explains only partially.

The most defensible reading is that both mechanisms operate, with weights that differ by field, level and institution. That is an unsatisfying conclusion, and it is probably the correct one.

## What it means for the current debate

The argument about whether universities are "worth it" typically assumes one model and argues from it. The more useful move is to ask which model dominates in a particular case — because the answer plausibly differs between a vocational qualification with a direct occupational route and a generalist degree whose value is partly in the sorting.

That is a harder question than the one usually asked. It is also the one that determines whether a given expansion of access does what its advocates hope.
