The shift from active to passive management is one of the few unambiguous wins for ordinary investors in modern finance. Fees collapsed. Diversification became trivial. A saver with no particular expertise can now hold a globally diversified portfolio for a few basis points a year — an outcome that would have seemed implausible a generation ago.

The interesting question is not whether this was good. It was. The question is what it does to the mechanism that sets prices in the first place.

## Index funds are price takers by construction

A passive fund does not form a view about whether a company is cheap. It buys in proportion to the index weight, which is itself a function of market price. Prices are therefore set at the margin, by whoever is still doing fundamental analysis — a shrinking share of total assets, though not necessarily a shrinking absolute amount of capital.

This is the crux of the debate. The pessimistic reading is that as the passive share rises, prices become less informative, capital is allocated worse, and mispricings persist for longer. The optimistic reading is that price discovery was never performed by the average dollar — it was performed by a relatively small set of informed participants, and that set remains large enough.

> Nobody has established the threshold at which the mechanism degrades. We are finding out empirically, in public, with real capital.

## What the evidence suggests so far

Three observations seem reasonably robust:

- **Correlation within indices has risen.** Stocks included in major indices move together more than they used to, which is consistent with flows mattering more than fundamentals at short horizons.
- **Active management has not disappeared.** It has concentrated. The capital doing genuine analysis is fewer, larger and more specialised.
- **Governance has migrated.** Voting power has concentrated in a handful of asset managers whose commercial incentive to engage on any individual company is weak relative to the size of their holdings.

The third point may matter more than the first two. Price discovery has a market mechanism that at least partly self-corrects: if prices become uninformative, the returns to becoming informed rise. Corporate governance has no equivalent. A dispersed owner base that is structurally disinclined to monitor management does not obviously fix itself.

## How to think about it

The useful posture is neither alarm nor dismissal. Passive investing is a genuine improvement in how households access markets, and it has introduced a structural question about who performs functions that used to be a by-product of active management. Both things are true, and the second deserves more attention than it typically receives.
