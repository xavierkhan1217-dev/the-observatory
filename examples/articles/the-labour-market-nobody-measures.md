Graduate outcome statistics are usually reported as employment rates: the share of graduates in work some months after finishing. It is a clean number, it is easy to collect, and it answers a question almost nobody is actually asking.

## Employment is the wrong threshold

The relevant question is not whether a graduate is working. It is whether they are doing work that uses what the degree was supposed to provide, at a wage that reflects it. A graduate in a job that does not require a degree is counted identically to one in a graduate-level role. Both appear as a success.

This matters because the policy debate about the value of higher education is conducted largely in terms of these figures. If the measure cannot distinguish between the two cases, it cannot support the conclusions people draw from it.

## Three things a better measure would capture

- **Match quality.** Does the role require the qualification held? Occupational classification gets closer to this than employment status does, though imperfectly.
- **Wage trajectory, not wage level.** Early-career earnings are noisy and heavily influenced by sector and geography. The slope matters more than the intercept.
- **Persistence.** Underemployment in the first year after graduation is common and often temporary. Underemployment still present at year five is a different phenomenon and should not be reported as the same thing.

> A statistic that cannot distinguish a research analyst from a graduate stacking shelves is not measuring the thing the debate is about.

## Why the gap persists

Partly because the better measures are harder and more expensive to collect. Partly because the simple number is institutionally convenient: it is reportable, comparable across institutions, and reliably flattering.

The result is a debate about whether degrees are worth it that runs on data unable to answer the question. That is not a reason to be cynical about higher education. It is a reason to be considerably more careful about what the published figures do and do not show — and a reasonably good argument for students collecting some of this evidence themselves.
