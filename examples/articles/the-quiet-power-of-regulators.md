Political argument concentrates on legislation because legislation is visible. Bills are debated, voted on and reported. But the rules that determine how a market actually operates — capital requirements, licensing conditions, merger thresholds, technical standards — are mostly written by regulatory agencies, under delegated authority, with limited public attention.

## Delegation is a decision, not an accident

The standard justification is credible commitment. Certain decisions are made worse by electoral pressure: a central bank that can be leaned on before an election makes different choices than one that cannot. Insulating those decisions produces better outcomes precisely because it removes them from immediate democratic control.

That argument is stronger than its critics allow. It is also narrower than its defenders often assume. It works well for decisions with a clear objective and a measurable target. It works much less well for decisions that involve genuine distributional trade-offs, where "technical expertise" is doing less work than it appears to.

> Independence solves a commitment problem. It does not dissolve the political content of the decision.

## Where the argument gets harder

Consider a regulator deciding how much risk a market should be permitted to carry. There is no purely technical answer. There is a trade-off between stability and credit availability, and different people are affected differently by where the line is drawn. Calling that judgement technical does not make it so; it simply relocates the politics somewhere less visible.

The useful question is not whether regulators have too much power in the abstract. It is whether, for each specific delegation, three conditions hold:

1. The objective is defined clearly enough that success and failure can be distinguished.
2. The agency reports transparently enough that an outsider could contest its reasoning.
3. There is a route by which a sustained democratic majority can change the mandate.

## The case for taking it seriously

Where those conditions hold, insulation is defensible and probably desirable. Where they do not, the arrangement is harder to justify — not because unelected officials are acting in bad faith, but because a contestable political choice has been placed somewhere that contestation is structurally difficult.

This is not an argument for abolishing independent agencies. It is an argument for being honest about what independence is for, and for noticing that the answer is different in different cases.
