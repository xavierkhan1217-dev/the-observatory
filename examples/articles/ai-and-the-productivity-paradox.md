In 1987 Robert Solow remarked that the computer age was visible everywhere except in the productivity statistics. The gap eventually closed — measured productivity growth accelerated in the mid-1990s, roughly a decade and a half after personal computers became common in offices.

The standard explanation is that general-purpose technologies require complementary investment before they show up in output. Firms had to reorganise around the technology, not merely install it. That takes time, costs money, and initially looks like expense rather than gain.

## The parallel is real but not exact

The case for expecting a similar lag today is straightforward. Organisational change is slow, the complementary investments are substantial, and early deployments tend to automate existing tasks rather than restructure the process around the new capability.

Two things are different this time, and they cut in opposite directions:

- **Deployment is faster.** Adoption does not require new hardware on every desk. That argues for a shorter lag.
- **The bottleneck may be organisational rather than technical.** Verification, liability and trust constrain where these systems can be used regardless of capability. That argues for a longer one.

> The computer analogy tells us the lag is normal. It does not tell us how long this one will be.

## What would count as evidence

The honest answer is that the aggregate statistics will not settle this for years, and firm-level evidence in the meantime is heavily selected — the firms that publish results are the ones with results worth publishing.

Two things would be more informative than another round of aggregate forecasts. First, whether gains persist beyond the pilot stage, or decay once the enthusiastic early users move on. Second, whether the gains concentrate at the frontier or diffuse. A technology that makes leading firms substantially more efficient while leaving the median firm unchanged produces a very different macroeconomic picture from one that raises the floor.

That distinction — frontier concentration versus genuine diffusion — is the one worth watching, and it is unfortunately the one that takes longest to observe.
