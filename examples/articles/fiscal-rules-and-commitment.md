A fiscal rule is an attempt to solve a time-inconsistency problem. A government would like to be believed when it says it will keep borrowing under control, because being believed lowers the cost of borrowing. But it also retains the discretion to spend more later, and everyone knows it. The rule exists to make the promise costly to break.

## Why most rules fail in the same way

The recurring pattern is that rules are calibrated for normal conditions and then encounter abnormal ones. A binding constraint on the deficit works until a recession, at which point adhering to it requires procyclical tightening — cutting spending precisely when demand is weakest. Governments then suspend the rule, which is usually the economically correct decision and always a blow to the rule's credibility.

The lesson is not that rules are pointless. It is that a rule which can only be honoured in good times is not really a commitment device; it is a statement of intent with extra steps.

> A constraint that gets suspended whenever it binds is not a constraint. It is a preference.

## What better design looks like

The features that distinguish durable frameworks are reasonably well understood:

- **State-contingent escape clauses**, defined in advance with explicit triggers, rather than suspension by announcement.
- **Targets on structural rather than headline balances**, so that automatic stabilisers are permitted to work.
- **An independent body** that assesses compliance but does not set the target, separating the technical judgement from the political one.
- **A defined path back**, so that invoking the clause carries an obligation rather than simply pausing the constraint.

## The underlying tension

None of this eliminates the core problem. A rule strong enough to genuinely bind a government is a rule that can produce the wrong macroeconomic policy in a severe downturn. A rule flexible enough to avoid that is a rule that can be reinterpreted when convenient.

The design question is therefore not how to remove discretion but where to locate it, and how visible to make its exercise. Frameworks that force a government to explain publicly why it is departing from its own rule do better than those that attempt to make departure impossible — not because transparency is a magic solution, but because it is the constraint that survives the situations rules are actually tested by.
