Undergraduate courses tend to compress *A Theory of Justice* into a single device: imagine choosing the rules of a society without knowing your place in it. Behind the veil of ignorance, the argument goes, you would not gamble on being at the bottom, so you would choose principles that protect the worst off.

Stated that way, the objections write themselves. Why assume that degree of risk aversion? Why should a hypothetical choice bind anyone who never made it? Both objections land against the compressed version. Neither lands as cleanly against what Rawls was actually doing.

## The device is about justification, not prediction

The original position is not a claim about what people would in fact choose. It is a constraint on what counts as a reason in political argument. Stripping away knowledge of your class, talents and conception of the good is a way of filtering out justifications that only work if you happen to be the person they benefit.

Read that way, the veil is less a decision procedure than a test: could this arrangement be defended to someone who does badly under it, in terms they could accept without simply being told they lost?

> A principle you would only endorse if you knew you were on the winning side is not a principle. It is a position.

## What survives the critiques

Three lines of attack have shaped the subsequent literature:

- **Communitarian.** The unencumbered self is a fiction; people's commitments are constitutive, not chosen. This is a serious objection to the moral psychology, less serious against the narrower claim about public justification.
- **Libertarian.** Nozick's argument that distributive patterns require continuous interference with liberty. Powerful against strong readings of the difference principle; weaker against the priority of basic liberties.
- **Capability-based.** Sen's argument that primary goods are the wrong metric, since people differ in their ability to convert resources into functioning. This one has largely been absorbed rather than defeated.

## Why it still does work

The reason Rawls remains on every syllabus is not that the conclusions are widely accepted — they are not. It is that he specified the standard of argument clearly enough that disagreement with him is productive. You can say precisely where you get off the train, and that precision is rarer in political philosophy than it should be.

Fifty years on, the durable contribution may be methodological rather than substantive: the insistence that a political arrangement owes a justification to the people who fare worst under it, stated in a form they could in principle accept.
