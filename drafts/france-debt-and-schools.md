French secondary schools have been in revolt since late September. What began in the Paris region spread nationwide within days, as pupils blockaded entrances and more than 400 schools closed, with clashes with riot police and in places burning vehicles and buildings. Arrest figures vary. The Interior Ministry cited around 2,000 early in the movement, of whom 90 per cent were minors, and a larger figure of 6,100 later. On 8 October more than 70,000 people marched, with university students and trade unions joining what had started as a schoolchildren's protest.

What the students are protesting about is specific and fairly mundane: crumbling buildings, long school days, and not enough teachers.

The government has moved quickly. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu suspended classes on 6 October, framing it as space for schools to discuss conditions internally, and observing that burning down a lycée does not improve it. Two days later the education minister promised the urgent deployment of 3,000 additional teachers, and Economy Minister Roland Lescure said there was room to meet the demands.

It is important to highlight that France is not cutting education. The 2027 budget gives schools 90.9 billion euros, an increase of 1.4 per cent, the largest rise of any department except defence. So pupils are protesting about decaying schools while the schools budget goes up.

France is not spending less on its schools. It is spending more on its debt, and the second is eating the first.

France will spend roughly 79 billion euros servicing its public debt this year. Next year the figure is forecast at 91 billion. That is more than the entire education budget.

The country now owes 3,595.5 billion euros, 119 per cent of GDP as of June, the highest since 1946, and projected to reach 121.7 per cent next year. The deficit will come in at around 5.4 per cent of GDP against a target of 4.6 per cent, and would reach 6.5 per cent next year if nothing were done. France borrows at roughly 4.5 per cent at some maturities, and the premium investors demand over German debt is the widest since 2012.

Debt service is not a line item like any other. It is the first claim on the budget and cannot be negotiated. It continues to grow whether or not the economy does.

> Every euro of it is a euro that cannot be spent on a roof, a teacher or a laboratory.

A nominal increase of 1.4 per cent for schools, in a budget where interest costs are rising by over 15 per cent, does not buy what it looks like it buys.

This is a fiscal squeeze seen from a classroom. Not a cut that headlines the news, announced and resisted, but a slow erosion in which spending technically rises and everything still gets worse.

The politics of stopping it are close to impossible. Consolidation asks people to accept visible pain now for a benefit that is invisible and arrives later: lower debt service in a decade, more room when the next crisis comes. Nobody takes to the streets demanding fiscal rectitude. They take to the streets when the ceiling leaks. So governments do what Lecornu's has just done, and announce a consolidation and then promise 3,000 teachers to make the protest stop.

France has been attempting this for years. Under its previous plan the deficit was to fall below the European Union's 3 per cent ceiling by 2029. The latest numbers are nearly a percentage point worse in both 2026 and 2027.

None of this means France is collapsing, and it is worth being clear about that. It remains one of the largest economies in the world, with an enormous tax base and no difficulty finding buyers for its bonds. The protests are not evidence of economic breakdown. They are evidence of accumulated underinvestment in buildings that have been deteriorating for years, which is a different and more gradual problem. There is also a serious argument that consolidating too aggressively into a weak economy is self-defeating, since cutting demand reduces the tax revenue that was supposed to close the gap.

The direction still matters. At 119 per cent of GDP and rising, with borrowing costs at 4.5 per cent, debt service compounds. Each year it takes a little more of the budget, which means each year the government has slightly less to spend on everything voters can actually see, which makes the politics of restraint slightly harder than the year before.

The students blockading their schools have identified something real. They have simply attributed it to the wrong thing. The money is not being withheld from them by an indifferent ministry. A growing share of it has already been committed, decades ago, to people who lent France money.
