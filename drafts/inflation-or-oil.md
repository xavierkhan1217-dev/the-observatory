On 16 September the Federal Reserve raised interest rates for the first time since 2023, lifting its target range by a quarter point to 3.75 to 4 per cent and reversing the cuts it had made the year before. The decision, taken after a two-day meeting of the Federal Open Market Committee, was unanimous. Kevin Warsh, sworn in as chair in May, has indicated that a further increase may come before the year is out. Markets put the odds of one in December at around three-quarters, down from where they stood before September's jobs report showed hiring slowing sharply.

The Fed is raising rates against a price rise it has no power to affect.

Strip out food and energy and the case for tightening looks weaker. Core inflation ran at 2.4 per cent in August against a target of 2 per cent. The Cleveland Fed's nowcast has it easing to 2.3 per cent by October, even as headline inflation climbs towards 3.6 per cent.

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level, better understood as a loss of purchasing power across the whole economy rather than a single price moving. An oil price rise is therefore not inflation in that sense but a relative price change, as energy becomes dearer compared to other goods. Either way households become poorer, but the cause differs and so must the policy used to correct it.

Rates work by raising the cost of borrowing, pushing firms to postpone investment and households to spend less, until the demand pressing against available supply weakens and prices ease. This is an effective mechanism when the problem is excess demand chasing too few goods. It is far less effective when the shortage lies on the supply side, because monetary policy can suppress demand but it cannot create supply.

> No interest rate has ever produced a barrel of oil.

Raising rates when the problem is energy hurts everything else in the economy and leaves the shortage exactly where it was. Growth slows, jobs go, and oil stays expensive. None of this means energy inflation is painless. It means that energy inflation lies largely beyond the reach of the instrument being used, which is why central banks usually look through supply shocks rather than fight them.

None of this is lost on the Fed. The committee is not debating whether oil is a supply shock but weighing what happens if it treats it as one. The vote was unanimous. A committee of this kind does not reach consensus on a weak case, and the case it is making is not really about oil at all.

The fear is a repeat of the 1970s, where an oil shock cut real wages, workers bargained to recover them, firms raised prices to cover the higher wage bill, and workers found themselves short again. Inflation stops being a one-off and settles into the general price level. Breaking it took until 1980, when Paul Volcker raised interest rates close to 20 per cent and the recession that followed defined a decade.

There is a second argument, made rather less openly, which is that raising rates works as a signal as much as a policy. If firms and households believe the Fed will always respond to inflation, they set wages and prices as though it is heading back to 2 per cent, and that expectation does much of the work on its own. That credibility costs little to maintain and a great deal to rebuild. Warsh, appointed after one of the closest confirmation votes in the Fed's history, has more reason than most to demonstrate that he will act.

A wage-price spiral is not a theory but a claim about what is happening in the labour market, and the labour market can be examined.

The numbers do not support it. Average hourly earnings rose 3 per cent in the year to September, down from 3.1 per cent in August and the slowest pace since May 2021. With inflation above 3 per cent, pay is falling behind prices. Payrolls grew by 29,000 in September, and the two months before were revised down.

Spirals require bargaining power. Workers can only extract compensating pay rises when labour is scarce and employers have no alternative. An economy adding 29,000 jobs a month, with pay growth at a five-year low, is not one in which that is happening. Rather than the first turn of a spiral, this looks more like the tail end of the inflation we have already had.

That reading could prove wrong, and it is worth saying how. If core inflation turns up over the winter, if wage growth re-accelerates, or if expectations drift from target, the Fed will have been early rather than mistaken, and waiting would have been the costlier error.

Until then the evidence points the other way. The inflation the Fed can actually control is close to where it wants it, while the inflation it is responding to comes out of the ground.
